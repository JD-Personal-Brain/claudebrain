## Summary
- **The core is "the algorithm":** five rules used in a fixed order. Question every requirement, delete, simplify, speed up, and only then automate. Running them out of order is the mistake it warns against.
- **Around it sit eight thinking habits:** reason from first principles, measure waste with the "idiot index", attack the limiting factor, treat the process as the product, iterate fast, act with urgency, cut bureaucracy, and seek negative feedback.
- **His own record shows the limits:** too much automation at Tesla in 2018 (he said so himself), timelines that slip year after year ("Elon time"), and deep cuts at Twitter that broke things.
- **Our read:** Musk treats a system as something to be stripped down before it is sped up, with the slowest part always getting the attention. The method is strong; the risks are speed without care and promises without dates that hold.
- **For us:** our machine already automates last and paces work by what blocks it. What Musk adds is subtraction: question and delete before automating, tag every requirement with where it comes from, and measure the machine itself. Eight suggestions below, all adopted and built on 2026-10-15.

## The core: "the algorithm" (five rules, in order)
Isaacson's biography describes this as Musk's method for cutting waste at Tesla and SpaceX; Musk also walked through it on camera on a 2021 tour of SpaceX's Starbase with the YouTuber Everyday Astronaut (Tim Dodd). The order matters: each rule assumes the one before it is done.

| # | Rule | What it means | The warning built into it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Make the requirements less dumb** | Question every requirement. Each one must come with the name of the person who set it, not a department, so it can be challenged. | Requirements from smart people are the most dangerous, because no one questions them. This applies to Musk's own requirements too. | ⚠️ |
| 2 | **Delete any part or process you can** | Remove steps, parts and approvals before improving anything. | If you never have to add something back, you didn't delete enough. Most summaries of the book give **10%** as the add-back rate; one report of Isaacson on a podcast gives **20%**. | ⚠️ (the number varies) |
| 3 | **Simplify and optimize** | Only now make what survived simpler and better. | "Possibly the most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist." | ⚠️ (said on the 2021 Starbase tour, by reports; exact wording not checked) |
| 4 | **Speed up the cycle time** | Make each loop faster, once rules 1 to 3 are done. | Speeding up a process that should have been deleted only makes waste faster. | ⚠️ |
| 5 | **Automate** | Automate last, once the process is lean and stable. | His 2018 Model 3 mistake is the example (below). | ⚠️ the rule · ✅ the 2018 admission |

**His own lesson on rule 5:** on April 13, 2018, during Model 3 production trouble, Musk posted that excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake, his mistake, and that "humans are underrated." He later told CBS the company had put too much new technology into the Model 3 at once. ✅

## The eight thinking habits around it

| Habit | What it means | Best-known example | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. First principles** | Break a problem down to what is physically true, then reason up from there, instead of copying what others do ("reasoning by analogy"). | 2012 interview with Kevin Rose: battery packs cost about $600 per kWh and most people assumed they always would. Musk asked what the materials are worth (cobalt, nickel, aluminum, carbon, polymers, a can) and put it at about $80 per kWh. In a 2013 TED talk he described the method as boiling things down to their fundamental truths and reasoning up from there. | ✅ the event · ⚠️ the dollar figures (from reports of the interview) |
| **2. Only physics is a hard limit** | Every requirement is a recommendation, except the laws of physics. | Isaacson quotes engineers on this; the line people repeat ("physics is the law, everything else is a recommendation") I could not find word for word. | ⚠️ |
| **3. The idiot index** | Divide what a finished part costs by what its raw materials cost. A high ratio means the design or the process is wasteful. | Isaacson's example: a $1,000 part made from $100 of aluminum (an index of 10). Musk's point: a high ratio means the design is idiotic (wording ⚠️). It dates to his 2002 trip to Russia to buy rockets. | ✅ the idea · ⚠️ the origin details |
| **4. The process is the product** | The factory, "the machine that builds the machine", is harder and has more room to improve than the product itself. | 2016: Musk said the room for improvement in making cars was at least ten times greater than in designing them (reports differ: ten times or a hundred times). 2021 Starbase tour: design is easy, making each part cheaply is the hard part. | ✅ the idea · ⚠️ the multiplier |
| **5. Attack the limiting factor** | At any moment, put the attention on whatever is slowest or scarcest, because nothing else moves the total. Tesla called its Model 3 bottleneck period "production hell." | Shows up across the biography and the Model 3 story; I did not find one defining quote. | ⚠️ |
| **6. Iterate fast and learn from failure** | Progress comes from the number of tries times what you learn from each, so build many versions and test them hard. | 2020: progress toward a production Starship depends on the number of prototype iterations times the progress between each. Satellite 2020 conference: without a dramatic rise in the rate of innovation, there is no chance of a city on Mars. "Failure is an option here. If things are not failing, you are not innovating enough" is widely credited to him. | ✅ the 2020 remarks · ❌ the failure quote (no original source found) |
| **7. Urgency and no bureaucracy** | Move as if time is the scarcest thing, and let information take the shortest path. | Isaacson: "a maniacal sense of urgency is our operating principle" (⚠️). April 2018 Tesla email, published by CNBC (✅): leave a meeting once you aren't adding value ("It is not rude to leave, it is rude to make someone stay and waste their time"), drop large meetings unless they add value to everyone, no made-up acronyms, and talk to whoever gets the job done fastest, not through the chain of command. | ✅ the email · ⚠️ the urgency line |
| **8. Feedback loops and negative feedback** | Always look for how to do things better, and ask for criticism, especially from friends. | Credited to him in many places: "It's very important to have a feedback loop, where you're constantly thinking about what you've done and how you could be doing it better." One source places it at a 2015 University of Southern California talk. | ❌ (no recording or transcript found) |

**How he says leaders should behave** (corollaries in Isaacson's book, through reviews; ⚠️ counts and wording vary): technical managers must do hands-on work (one summary gives about 20% of their time as an example); it's fine to be wrong, but not confidently wrong; hold skip-level meetings (meeting the people two levels down); don't let team loyalty stop people challenging each other; and every requirement has a named owner (rule 1).

## Where it went wrong: the other side
A fair point of view has to include the failures, many of them the method pushed too far.

| What happened | Which part of the method it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Automating too early.** The Model 3 line was heavily automated and fell behind; Musk called it his mistake (2018). | Rule 5 done before rules 1 to 4. | ✅ |
| **Timelines that slip ("Elon time").** He said in 2015 that self-driving cars were about three years away, promised a million robotaxis by 2020 (2019 investor day), and has set a new self-driving date almost every year since. A small trial robotaxi service started in Austin in June 2025. He calls himself "pathologically optimistic" and says he does deliver in the end. Investors coined "Elon time." | Urgency turned into public promises. | ✅ (Reuters, 2025; ⚠️ status since 2025 not checked) |
| **Deleting too deep at Twitter (late 2022).** About 3,700 jobs were cut with short notice, which led to a lawsuit; he shut down services he called "bloatware", and one of them handled two-factor sign-in, so some users couldn't log in. About six months later he reportedly said he would try to rehire some people (⚠️ not checked against a primary source). | Rule 2 without checking what each part did. | ✅ |
| **Pushing people hard.** Twitter staff were asked to commit to being "extremely hardcore," with reports of 80-hour weeks, and employees who criticized him were fired. Isaacson describes a dark streak that scared staff. | Urgency at the cost of the people and of honest feedback (habit 8). | ✅ the events · ⚠️ characterizations |

## Our read: how Musk engineers and improves a system
Pulled together from the above. This is our interpretation, not his words.
1. **Start from what is true, not what is done.** Physics and real costs set the limit; every other rule is a recommendation with a named owner, so it can be questioned.
2. **Subtract before you add.** Delete parts, steps and approvals until a few have to come back. That is how you know you cut enough.
3. **Measure the waste.** The idiot index compares what something costs with what it must cost. A big gap means a big opportunity.
4. **Work on the limiting factor.** The slowest step sets the pace of the whole system, so that is where attention goes, and it moves as each one is fixed.
5. **The process is the product.** The machine that builds the machine has more room to improve than the thing it makes.
6. **Shorten every loop.** More tries, faster, each one learning from the last, with failure in testing expected.
7. **Automate only what is lean and stable.** Automating a bad process locks in the waste; he learned this himself in 2018.
8. **Keep the feedback honest.** The loop works only if people can say what's wrong. His record at Twitter shows what happens when that breaks.

## What it means for our system: suggestions
Our product machine (the Product Workflow, Product Framework, Product Machine and Product Playbook) already shares much of Musk's thinking. Before these suggestions, its biggest gap in his terms was that it had a strong way to **add** (44 sub-steps, 33 general agents, 25 controls, an automation check aimed at moving work toward Auto) and no step that **deletes**. The automation check now cuts first. These suggestions are ours, not Musk's.

**Where we already think like him**

| Musk idea | Where it already lives in our system |
|---|---|
| Automate last (rule 5) | The automation check at every gate: a task moves toward Auto only when it repeats, is stable and is safe alone (Product Machine). |
| Attack the limiting factor | "The pace is set by data access and review, not build time" (Product Workflow); plans paced by what really blocks us (my profile). |
| Iterate fast | A working prototype in week 1; steps 3 to 6 repeat in short cycles (Product Workflow). |
| The process is the product | Sub-step 6.6, "Improve the machine itself" (Product Playbook). |

| Hands-on leaders | The product manager tests a rough mock-up with users and runs the reaction sessions (sub-step 1.6; AI drafts the mock-up). |

**Eight suggestions, strongest first (all adopted 2026-10-15)**

| # | Suggestion | Musk idea | Where it lives now | What it would let us do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Delete before you automate.** Put Musk's order in front of the automation check: does this task need to exist, and who required it? Can it be deleted? Simplified? Made faster? Law and bank-policy items are never cut. | Delete; add back about 10% (the figure most summaries give; one report says 20%) | The dry run, item PL1 in the Open Items log | A leaner machine before it meets the bank, instead of 44 sub-steps nobody has tested. |

Partner sign-offs don't change. | No meeting unless it adds value (2018 email) | "Gates in writing when they're clean" and the gate rows (Product Machine); the playbook's rules for every step | Fewer meetings; my hours go to the contested decisions. ⚠️ Whether the bank's culture accepts this is a guess. |

**Where Musk's method doesn't fit a bank** (guardrails for every suggestion above)
- **Law and the bank's policies are our physics.** Questioning a requirement never means removing a legal or policy control; suggestion 2 exists so those are marked and protected.
- **Know what a part does before you delete it.** Twitter lost two-factor sign-in that way. Every deletion goes in the decision log with what the part did and who agreed.
- **Fail in testing, never with clients.** Red-teaming and the test set are where failure is welcome; anything a client sees stays assistive first.
- **Urgency without "Elon time."** Give sponsors dates only when the limiting factor allows them, and keep unknown numbers as [X] instead of hopeful guesses.
- **A pace one person can keep.** The machine exists so I don't work 80-hour weeks; "hardcore" is the wrong lesson.
