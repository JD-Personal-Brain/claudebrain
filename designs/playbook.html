Delivery Playbook

How to run each of the six steps the first time, sub-step by sub-step, and what to watch so the second time can be automated. It sits under the delivery machine: that page says what happens; this one says how.

How to use it

Work through each sub-step, and log it as you go

Open the step you're in and work through its sub-steps in order. Each one has the same parts, so any step reads like any other. Fill in the first-time log as you go: you can't automate work you never wrote down.

Every sub-step has
  • Goal: what this sub-step is for, in one line.
  • Why it matters: what goes wrong if it's skipped.
  • Know first: the people, data and bank rules you need before starting.
  • First time, step by step: the actual work, in order.
  • Output: what it produces (a document, a number, a decision).
  • Done when: how to tell it's finished.
  • Automation watch: what to note while doing it, the agent it could become, the kind to use the first time and the kind to aim for (Auto, Draft or Me).
  • For RM analytics: a short worked example from the first project.
Rules for every step
  • No client data in an AI tool the bank hasn't approved, even for a quick test.
  • Guesses about the bank are marked ⚠️ until confirmed. Replace them with facts as I learn them, and note the source.
  • Numbers we don't have yet are [X]. Never invent them.
  • At the gate, run the automation check from product-framework.md on every Me and Draft task, and update the step's table there.

1.0

Kick off the project

Goal

Start with a named sponsor, the right people, a tracker and the risk partners told.

Why it matters

Without a named sponsor and agreed decision-makers, every later gate stalls on "who decides?"

Know first
  • Who is asking for this, and who owns the budget.
  • The bank's process for starting an AI project: is there an intake form or a review board? ⚠️ Not known yet.
  • Which tracker the bank uses for projects. ⚠️ Not known yet (see "Things to find out" in tech-stack.md).
Output

A one-line project statement, the who-decides list, the tracker, the risk contact briefed, the decision log.

Done when

The sponsor has confirmed their role, and every Discover task has an owner and a date.

First time, step by step
  1. Write one sentence about the project: "We want to [outcome] for [users], measured by [measure]."
  2. Meet the sponsor (30 minutes). Confirm that they own the outcome and the funding, and that they make the go/no-go call at each gate.
  3. Fill in "who's involved and who decides" (from product-framework.md) with real names. Mark gaps as [name needed].
  4. Set up the tracker: copy the six steps' task tables from product-framework.md, then give every Discover task an owner and a date.
  5. Brief a model risk contact in 15 minutes: what the idea is, what data it might use, and that nothing touches clients yet. Ask what they'll need to see in Define.
  6. Start the decision log and the risk/issue list (both empty is fine).
  7. Create the project file: memos/<project>.md.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me (sponsor and risk conversations) and Draft (setting up the tracker and files).
  • Note: how long setting up the tracker took, and what you copied by hand.
  • Agent idea: a kickoff pack builder that creates the tracker, decision log, project file and who-decides template from one project statement. Draft: you check it.
For RM analytics

Sponsor = the commercial banking leader. The statement: "more new revenue from RMs, by finding opportunities and freeing time to act on them." The memo's "Who's involved" table (section 3) is the who-decides list.

1.1

Frame the outcome with the sponsor

Goal

Agree what "success" means, in a measure the sponsor already cares about.

Why it matters

If the outcome is vague ("use AI more"), you can't size it, measure it or defend it at a gate.

Know first
  • The sponsor's own targets this year (revenue, efficiency, client numbers).
  • How the bank reports them today, and where (Salesforce, Power BI). ⚠️
Output

The outcome note: goal, levers, one measure each, out of scope.

Done when

The sponsor has confirmed the note in writing (an email reply is enough).

First time, step by step
  1. Ask the sponsor: "If this works, what number moves, and by how much in a year?" Write their words down exactly.
  2. List the possible levers. Each is one way the outcome could move.
  3. Agree on one measure per lever, using a number the bank already tracks where possible.
  4. Agree on what is out of scope, such as credit decisions, which carry a much higher legal bar (see Governance in tech-stack.md).
  5. Write a one-page outcome note and send it to the sponsor to confirm.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me. Agreeing a goal is a conversation.
  • Note: which questions got the most useful answers. They become a standard question list.
  • Agent idea: an outcome note drafter that turns the meeting recap into the one-page note (M365 Copilot). Draft.
For RM analytics

Two goals (more revenue opportunities, more time to act) and three levers (win new clients, grow existing clients, keep clients), as in memo section 1.

1.2

Size the opportunity with real data

Goal

Put a rough dollar figure on each lever, so you know which one is biggest.

Why it matters

Without sizing, the first target is picked by opinion, and the value model in Define has nothing to stand on.

Know first
  • Where the data lives (likely Salesforce for pipeline and win/loss, and Snowflake for balances and products ⚠️) and who owns it.
  • What you're allowed to query, and with which tools. Ask the data owner before running anything.
  • An analyst who can check the numbers.
Output

A sizing table (lever, question, answer, dollar range, confidence, query used).

Done when

Every lever has a range and the biggest numbers have been checked by someone else.

First time, step by step
  1. Write each lever as a plain question. Example: "How many clients have product A but not product B?"
  2. Ask the data owner for access, or ask an analyst to run the queries.
  3. Run each question and save the query alongside its answer.
  4. Turn each answer into a rough dollar range: count × typical revenue × a realistic hit rate. Use [X] where you don't have a number yet.
  5. Have the analyst re-run the two or three biggest numbers.
  6. Rank the levers by size, and note how confident you are in each.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft. AI can run first-pass questions; a person checks them.
  • Note: which questions you asked. They will repeat on every project with a revenue goal.
  • Agent: A8 Opportunity sizer (memo Appendix A). It answers plain-English sizing questions read-only, on Snowflake Cortex Analyst. An analyst re-runs the key numbers.
For RM analytics

Size new clients (prospects that look like the best clients), growth (product gaps such as treasury management) and retention (falling balances). Use Salesforce pipeline and Snowflake balances.

1.3

Interview and shadow users

Goal

Learn how users really work today, and where it hurts.

Why it matters

Data shows what happens; only people tell you why, and what they'd actually use.

Know first
  • Who to talk to, by role. See job-notes.md, Part 1, step 1.
  • How many: [X] per role. ⚠️ A common starting point is about 5 to 8 per role. Stop when you stop hearing new things.
  • The bank's rules on recording meetings and on using AI recaps. ⚠️ Confirm before recording.
Output

Interview notes, shadowing notes, and a themes list with quotes and sources.

Done when

You've covered every role and the last two or three interviews added nothing new.

First time, step by step
  1. Write a 30-minute interview guide of 8 to 10 open questions. Use prompting.md Example 1 to draft it, for example "Walk me through what you did after your last client meeting."
  2. Ask user leaders to nominate a mix of people: strong performers, average ones and skeptics.
  3. Send invitations and book the interviews.
  4. Run each interview. Listen more than you talk, and ask "show me" rather than "tell me".
  5. Shadow two or three people for half a day each, and write down every task and how long it takes.
  6. After each one, write a 5-line summary the same day: biggest pain, a quote, and what surprised you.
  7. When done, pull out themes, with each theme linked to the interviews behind it.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me for the conversations and the scheduling. Draft for themes.
  • Note: how long scheduling took (it usually takes more time than you expect), and whether your guide changed after the first few interviews.
  • Agents:
    • A7 Interview synthesizer (Appendix A): turns recaps into themes linked to their sources. You check every theme.
    • New: an interview scheduler (Power Automate with Outlook) that sends invitations and reminders to nominated users. Auto, once the bank allows automations to message people ⚠️.
For RM analytics

Talk to RMs, their sales managers, and treasury specialists and credit analysts. The key question: "How do you decide which client to call next?"

1.4

Map where their time goes

Goal

A picture of the user's week: what takes time, what's admin, and what leads to results.

Why it matters

"banker capacity" claims need a baseline. You can't say you saved time without knowing where it went.

Know first
  • Activity data: Salesforce activities, and M365 Copilot or Viva Insights reports for email and meetings. ⚠️ Check what the bank has turned on.
  • Your shadowing notes from 1.3.
Output

A time map (activity, hours per week, type, source: data or estimate).

Done when

Users recognize it as their week.

First time, step by step
  1. List the main activities from shadowing, such as meeting prep, client meetings, call notes, CRM updates and internal meetings.
  2. Pull the data you have for each one: counts and time where the data allows it.
  3. Fill the gaps with estimates from the interviews, labeled as estimates.
  4. Mark each activity as client-facing, admin or internal.
  5. If the data allows it, check which activities show up before won deals.
  6. Show the map to two or three users: "Does this look like your week?" Fix it.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: which data sources were useful, and which were empty or untrusted.
  • Agent: A9 Time-use analyst (Appendix A). It reads Salesforce activity read-only. You confirm the results with users in person.
For RM analytics

The memo targets meeting prep, call notes and CRM updates as the admin to cut.

1.5

Understand how they're measured and rewarded

Goal

Know whether using the new tool would help users hit their own goals.

Why it matters

People adopt what helps them get paid or recognized. A tool that creates work their scorecard ignores won't get used.

Know first
  • Who owns the scorecards and incentives (sales leadership, HR).
  • What's confidential: you may only get the structure, not individual numbers.
Output

A short note: what users are measured on, whether it matches the outcome, and any gap.

Done when

The sponsor knows about any gap and has said how it will be handled.

First time, step by step
  1. Ask sales leadership: "What are users measured on, and what's paid?"
  2. Ask users the same question in interviews, and compare the answers.
  3. Check whether the outcome from 1.1 shows up in their scorecard.
  4. If it doesn't, write down the gap and raise it with the sponsor now, not at launch.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me. These are sensitive conversations.
  • Note: who had the real answer. Next project, go to them first.
  • Agent idea: none. This stays with a person.
For RM analytics

If RMs are paid on closed revenue but not on referrals, cross-team referral insights will be ignored. Raise that early.

1.6

Test a rough mock-up

Goal

Get real reactions to something users can see and click, before anything is built.

Why it matters

People can't tell you what they want in the abstract, but they react instantly to a screen.

Know first
  • The top 2 or 3 pains from 1.3 and 1.4.
  • What data you may show. Use sample data only, with no real client data.
  • Where to build it: a clickable HTML prototype (our prototyping process in NOW.md), or Copilot Studio or Power Apps in a test environment.
Output

The prototype versions and a reaction log (version, user, what they did, what changed).

Done when

Users can say what they'd use it for in their own words, or you've learned it's the wrong problem.

First time, step by step
  1. Pick the one pain the mock-up should solve.
  2. Build a rough version in a day or two. Rough is fine.
  3. Show it to 3 to 5 users. Ask "What would you do next?", not "Do you like it?"
  4. Write down what they did, what confused them and what they asked for.
  5. Change it and show it again a few days later. Repeat 2 or 3 times.
  6. Keep every version, as in the prototyping process.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft for the mock-up (Claude builds it with the prototype skill). Me for running the sessions and summarizing reactions.
  • Note: how long each version took to build, and what feedback came up every time.
  • Agent idea: a reaction summarizer that turns session notes into "what to change next". Draft.
For RM analytics

A pre-meeting client brief and a weekly opportunity list, built on sample clients.

1.7

Pick the first target and record today's numbers

Goal

Choose what to build first, and lock in the "before" numbers it will be measured against.

Why it matters

Without a baseline, you can't prove impact later, and the value model is guesswork.

Know first
  • Everything from 1.1 to 1.6.
  • Where the baseline numbers will come from (Power BI or Snowflake), and who owns them.
Output

The recommendation, the recorded baseline, the proposed comparison group, the first value model and the gate pack.

Done when

The baseline is recorded with its source, and the gate pack is ready.

First time, step by step
  1. List the candidate targets. Score each on size (1.2), pain (1.3 and 1.4), user reaction (1.6), data readiness and risk.
  2. Recommend one, with the reason and the runners-up.
  3. For its measure, record today's number, the date, the source and the exact definition.
  4. Propose a comparison group: similar users who won't get the tool at first. (Finance agrees the method in 2.7; the final group is confirmed in 2.8.)
  5. Write a first rough value model with [X] placeholders, using memo section 2 as the pattern.
  6. Build the gate pack: the outcome note, the sizing table, themes, the time map, the incentive note, reactions, the recommendation and the baseline.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me for the choice and for recording the baseline by hand. Draft for scoring and the gate pack.
  • Note: which parts of the gate pack you copied from earlier sub-steps. That copying can be automated.
  • Agent ideas:
    • A baseline recorder (Power BI) that snapshots the measure on a schedule from today on. Auto.
    • A gate pack drafter that pulls each sub-step's outputs into one document. Draft.
For RM analytics

Wave 1 = pre-meeting briefs, meeting follow-ups and the weekly opportunity list (memo Step 1).

Go / no-go

The Discover gate

  • Who decides: the sponsor, with a risk partner present.
  • Bring: the gate pack from 1.7.
  • Go means: the sponsor agrees on the target, the measure and today's baseline. The risk partner has no blocking concern.
  • No-go means: go back to the sub-step that fell short (usually 1.2 or 1.7), or stop the project and record why.
  • Record: the decision, who decided and why, in the decision log.

2.1

Write the problem and the outcome

Goal

One sentence for the problem and one for the measurable outcome.

Why it matters

Every later choice (scope, data, tests) is checked against these two sentences. If they're fuzzy, scope creeps.

Know first

The Discover gate pack, especially the chosen target, the baseline and the users' own words.

Output

The first lines of the product brief.

Done when

The sponsor agrees, and a user would say "yes, that's my problem."

First time, step by step
  1. Problem: "[Users] lose / miss [what], because [cause], shown by [evidence from Discover]."
  2. Outcome: "[Measure] moves from [baseline] to [target] by [date] for [pilot group]." Use [X] if the target isn't agreed yet.
  3. Read both aloud to two users and the sponsor. Do they recognize them?
  4. Put them at the top of the one-page product brief.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: how many rewrites it took, and why.
  • Agent: A10 Requirements drafter (memo Appendix A) drafts both sentences from the Discover findings. You edit, and the sponsor approves.
For RM analytics

"RMs miss growth opportunities in their existing book because signals are scattered across systems," with the outcome tied to AI-sourced pipeline.

2.2

Choose the platform and delivery path

Goal

Decide where the product will live and how it gets from test to live.

Why it matters

The platform decides what's possible, what it costs, which approvals apply and how fast you can pilot.

Know first
  • Where users spend their day (from 1.3 and 1.4).
  • The "pick the layer" rule of thumb in tech-stack.md.
  • Platform team contacts for Salesforce, Microsoft and AWS.
  • IT's data rules (DLP) for Power Platform connectors. ⚠️ Check these early, because they often block ideas.
Output

The platform decision with its scores, and the delivery path.

Done when

The platform team agrees it's feasible, and the decision is logged.

First time, step by step
  1. Score the candidate platforms on five criteria: where users work, where the data lives, fit with approvals and controls, cost model, and speed to a pilot.
  2. Meet each platform team for 30 minutes: "Could we do X? What would block us?"
  3. Write the delivery path: build environment → test → live, who moves it, and what approval each move needs.
  4. Record the choice and the runner-up in the decision log.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me, with platform teams.
  • Note: the questions each platform team asked. They become a standard feasibility checklist.
  • Agent idea: a platform fit checker that scores a brief against the five criteria and lists known blockers. Draft.
For RM analytics

Start on the Salesforce client record (Agentforce, Prompt Builder), with a Monday digest through Power Automate (memo section 5).

2.3

Define the data contract

Goal

Write down exactly which data the product uses, how fresh it must be and how it's checked.

Why it matters

Most AI projects slip on data. A signed contract means no feature depends on data you don't have.

Know first
  • Data owners for each source (Snowflake, Salesforce).
  • Which tables are trusted ("certified") and who may see what. ⚠️ Ask the data team how certification works at the bank.
Output

The data contract table and a gap list.

Done when

Every field is either confirmed or has an agreed fix, and the owner has signed.

First time, step by step
  1. From the Discover target and the 1.6 mock-up screens, list a first set of fields the product needs. Update the list when the user stories (2.5) are done.
  2. For each field: source, owner, refresh rate needed, refresh rate today, quality check, and who may see it.
  3. Check that each field exists and is fresh enough. Mark the gaps.
  4. Agree with the data owner how each gap is fixed or worked around.
  5. Get the data owner's sign-off, in writing.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: how long the sign-off took, and which gaps came up. Expect the same gaps on the next project.
  • Agent: A11 Data contract checker (Appendix A). It checks fields, freshness and quality read-only, and the data owner signs off.
For RM analytics

Balances, product holdings and trends from Snowflake; client records, pipeline and activity from Salesforce ⚠️ (where each lives is still to confirm).

2.4

Start the risk intake and list the control requirements

Goal

Get an early risk rating, and know which controls you must build in.

Why it matters

Controls are much cheaper to build in from the start than to bolt on at step 4. An early rating also tells you how long approval will take.

Know first
  • The bank's AI or model risk intake process and form. ⚠️ Not known yet.
  • Your risk contact from 1.0.
  • The governance basics in tech-stack.md (SR 11-7, privacy, fair lending).
Output

The submitted intake, an early rating, and a list of control requirements.

Done when

The risk partner has given an early rating and the control list.

First time, step by step
  1. Get the intake form and ask your risk contact how long a review usually takes.
  2. Fill it in from the brief so far (2.1) and the Discover gate pack: what the product does, which data (the first field list from 2.3), who uses it, what it can change, and whether anything reaches a client.
  3. Say plainly what it does not do, for example "no credit decisions, no pricing."
  4. Submit it, then meet the risk partner to walk through it.
  5. Write down the control requirements they give you (human approval, logging, guardrails, permissions) and copy them into the backlog as must-haves.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft. You fill it in; risk rates it.
  • Note: which questions on the form were hard to answer. Next time, answer them earlier in Discover.
  • Agent: A12 Risk intake assistant (Appendix A). It pre-fills the intake and flags likely high-risk areas, and the risk partner sets the rating.
For RM analytics

Any insight that suggests a credit product goes to compliance review, because fair-lending rules apply to business credit too.

2.5

Write user stories and acceptance criteria

Goal

Turn the problem into small, testable pieces of work.

Why it matters

The stories are what gets built, and their acceptance criteria are how you know each piece is done.

Know first

The brief (2.1), the Discover themes, and the control requirements (2.4).

Output

A backlog of stories with acceptance criteria.

Done when

Every story in the first release has criteria that a tester could check.

First time, step by step
  1. Write each story as "As a [user], I need [what], so that [why]."
  2. Under each, add 2 to 4 acceptance criteria: "Given [situation], when [action], then [result]."
  3. Add control stories once the control list comes back from 2.4, for example "As a risk partner, I need every AI action logged."
  4. Review them with two users and one engineer: are they clear, and are they buildable?
  5. Put them in the backlog tool. ⚠️ Which tool the bank uses isn't known yet.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: which story patterns repeat, such as permissions, logging and feedback. These become a story library for every project.
  • Agent: A10 Requirements drafter (Appendix A).
For RM analytics

"As an RM, each Monday I need my top 5 client opportunities, with the reason and a suggested next step, so I can plan my calls."

2.6

Set priorities and record the trade-offs

Goal

Rank the backlog and write down why.

Why it matters

Partners will ask "why not X?" A recorded trade-off answers that once, instead of in every meeting.

Know first

The stories (2.5), rough effort from engineers, and risk from 2.4.

Output

A ranked backlog and decision-log entries.

Done when

The sponsor agrees with the top of the list.

First time, step by step
  1. Score each story on value, effort and risk (high, medium or low is enough).
  2. Rank them. Must-have controls go first, whatever their score.
  3. For each hard call, write a decision-log entry: what was chosen, what was given up, who decided and why.
  4. Review the ranking with the sponsor.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me. Trade-offs are judgment calls.
  • Note: which scoring questions actually changed the order.
  • Agent idea: a decision log drafter that writes log entries from meeting recaps (M365 Copilot). Draft.
For RM analytics

Briefs, follow-ups and the opportunity list come first; call notes saved to Salesforce wait for wave 2.

2.7

Set the measures, quality bar and value model

Goal

Decide how success is measured, how good is good enough, and whether it pays for itself.

Why it matters

This is what the Release and Measure gates will judge against. Set it now, before results come in, so no one moves the goalposts.

Know first
  • The five kinds of measures and the quality bar in product-framework.md.
  • The baseline and the first value model from 1.7.
  • A Finance contact, to agree how impact is counted.
Output

The measures sheet, the quality bar, the value model, and the method Finance agreed.

Done when

The sponsor and Finance agree, and every measure has a source.

First time, step by step
  1. Pick one or two measures for each of the five kinds: usage, quality, business impact, adoption, risk and controls.
  2. Set the quality bar thresholds: accuracy, sources shown, relevance, usefulness, and permissions (zero tolerance). Use [X] until agreed.
  3. Fill in the value model with real numbers where you have them, and [X] where you don't.
  4. Agree with Finance how impact is counted: how wins are tagged, and how the comparison group is used.
  5. Define each measure exactly (source, formula, owner), so the dashboard can be built from it.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: which measures you could pull automatically and which needed someone by hand.
  • Agent idea: a measures sheet builder that drafts the measures and their sources from the brief and the data contract. Draft.
For RM analytics

Every AI-sourced opportunity is tagged in Salesforce from day one, and Finance agrees the comparison method before the pilot (memo section 2).

2.8

Scope the first release and the pilot group

Goal

Pick the smallest useful version and who tries it first.

Why it matters

A small first release reaches users sooner and teaches you more than a big one.

Know first

The ranked backlog (2.6), the measures (2.7), and user leaders' input on who should pilot.

Output

The release 1 scope, the pilot and comparison groups, a date, the long-term owner (or a date to decide), the finished brief and the gate pack.

Done when

The gate pack is ready.

First time, step by step
  1. Draw a line in the backlog: above it is release 1.

  2. Set up a channel (for example in Teams) where champions share tips.
  3. Collect early wins: a deal, an hour saved or a client moment that started with the product.
  4. Ask leaders to recognize those wins in their own meetings.

5.6

Fix fast

Goal

Act on feedback every week, and show users that you did.

Why it matters

Users keep giving feedback only if they see it acted on.

Know first

Where feedback lands (from 3.5) and who can fix what.

Output

A weekly fix list, release notes and the incident log.

Done when

This runs every week.

First time, step by step
  1. Read all feedback every week.
  2. Sort it into bugs, quality issues and ideas.
  3. Fix the top items, and add the rest to the backlog.
  4. Tell users what changed ("you said, we did").
  5. Log every incident, its owner and the fix.
Automation watch
  • First time: Me, reading everything yourself the first few weeks.
  • Note: how feedback splits between bugs, quality and ideas, and how long sorting took.
  • Agent: A17 Feedback triage (Appendix A) sorts and routes it, and you review a weekly sample. Aim for Auto.
For RM analytics

Feedback logged in Salesforce from the one-click buttons.

5.7

Scale in waves

Goal

Expand to more users only after a go/no-go check.

Why it matters

Scaling something that isn't working spreads the problem.

Know first

The pilot results against the targets from 2.7.

Output

The scale recommendation, the confirmed long-term owner and, on a go, the next wave plan.

Done when

The sponsor has decided.

First time, step by step
  1. Compare pilot results with the targets: weekly use, results, incidents.
  2. Write a one-page scale recommendation: go, wait or change, with the next wave and its date.
  3. Confirm the long-term owner recorded in 2.8. If it's still to be decided, the sponsor names one now: scaling past the pilot needs a named owner.
  4. Take it to the sponsor (this is the gate).
  5. On a go, repeat 5.1 to 5.6 for the next wave, reusing everything from this one.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft for the recommendation, Me for the decision.
  • Note: what you reused from wave 1. The more you reuse, the faster later waves go.
  • Agent idea: the gate pack drafter, reused. Draft.
For RM analytics

Expand only after the go/no-go check on the adoption dashboard.

Go / no-go

The Release and adopt gate

  • Who decides: the sponsor, with user leaders.
  • Bring: the pilot results and the scale recommendation.
  • Go means: weekly use and results hit the targets, with no serious incidents, and the long-term owner confirmed before scaling.
  • No-go means: fix the routine, the product or the training, then check again.
  • Record: the decision in the decision log.

Track the measures on a live dashboard

Goal

Extend the pilot dashboard (built in 5.1) to all five kinds of measures, updated automatically.

Why it matters

Output

Done when

It refreshes by itself and matches the sources.

First time, step by step

6.3

Re-test after every change, and watch for drift

Goal

Catch quality slipping before users do.

Why it matters

AI quality can slip over time, as data, models or prompts change ("drift"). Users stop trusting a tool after a few bad answers.

Know first

The test set (3.6) and the monitoring plan (4.8).

Output

The score trend and any alerts acted on.

Done when

This runs on every change without anyone remembering to do it.

First time, step by step
  1. Re-run the full test set after every change, and at least monthly.
  2. Compare the score with the last run and with the quality bar.
  3. Watch the live signals: rejection rate, guardrail blocks and user overrides.
  4. If a threshold is crossed, follow the monitoring plan: fix, roll back or pause.
Automation watch
  • First time: Draft.
  • Note: what caused each drop.
  • Agent: A19 Quality watchdog (Appendix A) re-runs the test set and alerts. The product manager decides whether to fix or roll back. Aim for Auto.
For RM analytics

Watch RM rejections of insights; a rising rate is the earliest warning.

6.4

Hold the monthly keep / change / stop review

Goal

A monthly decision on each feature, based on evidence.

Why it matters

Without a regular decision, weak features linger and strong ones don't get more investment.

Know first

The dashboard (6.1), the impact note (6.2) and quality trends (6.3).

Output

The decisions, recorded, and an updated backlog.

Done when

Every feature has a decision for the month.

First time, step by step
  1. Build a short pack: each feature's use, quality and impact, plus the top user feedback.
  2. Recommend keep, change or stop for each one, with the reason.
  3. Meet the sponsor (30 minutes) and decide.
  4. Record the decisions and update the backlog.
Automation watch
